# THE CASE

## 1. A Monday Morning in Bielefeld

At 8:15 on a Monday morning in September 2026, Dr. Katharina Brune, Chief Executive of Sparkasse Ostwestfalen, stared at a single number on the screen in the boardroom on Kesselbrink: **57**.

That was the median age of the bank's active main-bank customers — the people whose salary or pension actually lands in a Sparkasse account each month. Below it, CFO Markus Tegeler had placed a second figure. In 2025, Sparkasse Ostwestfalen had opened roughly 1,900 new main-bank relationships with people under 25. In the same year, by the bank's own estimate, the three largest neobanks had opened around 11,000 accounts in the bank's territory in that same age group.

"We are not losing a product line," Tegeler said. "We are losing a generation. And with it, quietly, the funding base and the mortgage pipeline of the 2030s."

The Management Board had agreed on one thing before the meeting ended: at the October strategy session, it wanted to see a concrete, costed, prioritised programme of **service innovations to win back customers under 30** — not another brand campaign, and not a vague digital vision. Dr. Brune has engaged your team to prepare that recommendation. You have three hours before you present to the full board.

## 2. Sparkasse Ostwestfalen: A Public Bank with a Long Memory

Sparkasse Ostwestfalen ("SPK OWL") is a German public-law savings bank (*Anstalt des öffentlichen Rechts*), formed in 2019 through the merger of three district savings banks in the Ostwestfalen region of North Rhine-Westphalia. Its owners (*Träger*) are a joint municipal body formed by the City of Bielefeld and two surrounding districts. Its business territory (*Geschäftsgebiet*) covers roughly 1.04 million inhabitants: the city of Bielefeld, a ring of mid-sized towns, and a prosperous rural hinterland dense with family-owned *Mittelstand* manufacturers.

Key facts (2025, case data): total assets of €11.3 billion, customer deposits of €8.9 billion, customer loans of €7.6 billion, roughly 528,000 retail customers, about 1,720 full-time-equivalent employees, and 79 branches — down from 118 at the time of the merger. Operating profit before tax in 2025 was €59.7 million (Exhibit 3).

Three structural features matter for any strategy discussion:

- **The public mandate.** Under the Savings Bank Act of North Rhine-Westphalia, SPK OWL exists to provide banking services to the whole population of its territory, to promote saving, and to support the regional economy. Profit is necessary, but it is not the purpose. The *Verwaltungsrat* — chaired by a local mayor and including council politicians and employee representatives — watches branch coverage and community presence closely.
- **The regional principle (*Regionalprinzip*).** SPK OWL may not actively acquire customers outside its territory. Its total addressable market for the under-30 segment is therefore fixed: approximately **158,000 residents aged 16-29**, including around 38,000 students at the region's universities (Universität Bielefeld, HSBI, TH OWL in Lemgo) and roughly 29,000 apprentices in the *Mittelstand* and the skilled trades.
- **The group IT backbone.** Like nearly all German savings banks, SPK OWL runs on the shared core banking platform and the shared "Sparkasse" mobile app provided centrally by the group's IT provider, Finanz Informatik (FI). The app itself is competitive and well rated, but local customisation is limited and major feature changes depend on group-wide release cycles of 12-18 months. The bank can, however, build satellite services, local modules, and partnerships on top of the platform, and it can shape its own pricing, service model, and regional presence freely.

SPK OWL also distributes products of the Sparkassen-Finanzgruppe's partner companies: Deka (funds and securities), LBS (building savings), and a public insurer. Securities commissions — mostly Deka funds — have been one of the few growing fee lines (Exhibit 3).

## 3. Market Context: German Retail Banking and the Neobank Wave

*(All figures in this section are case data.)*

German retail banking remains crowded and structurally low-margin: around 1,350 banks, of which about 340 are savings banks and roughly 700 are cooperative banks. The national branch network has fallen from about 28,000 branches in 2014 to under 19,500 in 2025. Cash still matters — about 45% of point-of-sale transactions by count in 2025 — but its share falls every year, and the girocard, Apple Pay, and Google Pay now dominate younger customers' wallets. Since 2025, EU regulation has made instant SEPA payments a standard rather than a premium feature, and the European payment wallet Wero, backed by German banks including the savings bank group, is trying to establish itself against PayPal.

The interest-rate environment has normalised: after the 2022-23 hiking cycle, the ECB deposit rate stood at 2.0% in mid-2026. This matters twice over. First, deposits are valuable again: the bank's internal transfer pricing currently credits sight deposits with a margin of about 1.7%, expected to normalise toward 1.4% by 2028. Second, rates gave neobanks a marketing weapon: several pass interest on balances directly to customers, which incumbent banks with branch cost structures find painful to match.

The disruptive story of the decade, however, is the **neobank and direct-bank wave among the young**. In Germany (case data, early 2026): Trade Republic serves about 4.6 million customers, having grown from a broker into a licensed bank offering 2.0% interest on deposits and a cashback card; N26 has about 4.3 million German customers; Revolut roughly 2.1 million and rising fast; C24 about 1.1 million; and ING Deutschland, the largest direct bank, about 9.6 million. Surveys suggest more than 40% of Germans aged 18-29 hold at least one account with a neobank or direct bank, and for a growing share it is the main account. Their common formula: account opening in under ten minutes, no monthly fee, an app built around instant notifications and card control, free or near-free ETF savings plans, and a brand voice that speaks to twenty-year-olds without embarrassment.

Crucially for this case, the neobanks' weaknesses are also well documented (Exhibits 4 and 5): no cash infrastructure, no local presence, thin human service, and limited trust when stakes get high. Sixty-two per cent of young people in SPK OWL's own survey say they would trust the Sparkasse most "with my salary and savings in a crisis" — yet they do not bank there.

## 4. The Problem in Numbers

Between 2019 and 2025, SPK OWL's customers aged 16-29 fell from 95,700 to 73,300 — a net loss of roughly 3,700 per year, in a period when the region's young population was broadly stable (Exhibit 1). Worse than the headline is the quality of what remains: among account holders aged 20-29, the share whose salary or main regular inflow arrives at the Sparkasse fell from 58% in 2019 to 37% in 2025. Many young "customers" keep a near-dormant account for cash deposits or out of inertia while living financially at Trade Republic or N26.

Two mechanical drivers stand out in the bank's own data:

- **The 18th-birthday cliff.** SPK OWL still wins children: parents open most youth accounts, often alongside the traditional *Weltspartag* savings rituals. But 78% of youth accounts are opened by parents, and the relationship belongs to the parent, not the child. Of the roughly 3,400 youth accounts that reach the holder's 18th birthday each year, only 59% are still active two years later — down from 83% in 2019 (Exhibit 8). Of those who lapse, two-thirds open their new main account at a neobank or direct bank.
- **A leaky front door.** The bank's online account opening exists, but of applications started, only 58% survive the video identification step and just 34% end as a funded, card-activated account within 60 days. The median time from application to a usable card is seven days. The neobank benchmark is minutes to a virtual card and roughly 61% funded within 60 days (Exhibit 8).

The financial consequence is not yet visible in the P&L — 2025 was still a solid year — and that is exactly what worries Tegeler. Customers under 30 hold only 4.8% of deposits, and on a one-year view many are barely profitable (Exhibit 6). But customers under 35 provide roughly one euro in eleven of the deposits that fund the loan book, and 71% of today's 30-44-year-old mortgage borrowers — the bank's most valuable retail relationships — were already customers before their 25th birthday. The under-30 franchise is the pipeline for everything the bank earns money on later. It is eroding at a compounding rate.

## 5. A Scar That Still Itches: The "YoungS" Pilot

This is not the bank's first attempt. In 2018 the predecessor institutions launched **"YoungS"**, a standalone youth banking app with its own brand, built by an external agency outside the FI platform. It offered a stripped-down account, stickers, and a cashback scheme with two local cinema chains. It reached 4,100 downloads, converted fewer than 900 accounts, suffered from a clunky duplicate login, and was quietly discontinued in 2020 after cumulative spend of €2.1 million. Several board members draw a simple lesson — "youth apps do not work." Jonas Willeke, Head of Digital Channels, draws a different one — "half-serious side projects do not work." Your recommendation will land in the middle of that unresolved argument.

## 6. Voices from Inside and Outside

**Dr. Katharina Brune, CEO (54):** "I do not want a Sparkasse that is a museum with a treasury attached. But I also refuse to chase Trade Republic into a price war we cannot win. Our advantages are trust, physical presence, and the fact that we are *from here*. The question is whether any of that can be made to matter to a 22-year-old — in her pocket, not in a brochure."

**Markus Tegeler, CFO (57):** "Every euro we spend on the under-30s is a euro of today's profit spent on a hypothesis about 2035. I can defend that to the *Verwaltungsrat* — once. So bring me a business case, not a mood board: costs, breakeven, payback, and what we stop doing if it fails. And remember our cost-income ratio is drifting toward 70% while our guardrails say we hold it below 68% by 2029."

**Sandra Okafor, Head of Retail (46):** "Do not let anyone tell you branches are the enemy. Our survey says 44% of young people still value cash access, and the moment a customer faces a real decision — first apartment, first securities account, an inheritance — a human being becomes our conversion weapon. The problem is that nothing in our current model *brings* a 23-year-old to that human being."

**Jonas Willeke, Head of Digital Channels (34):** "Our app is genuinely good — better than its reputation with people who have never opened it. What we lack is everything around it: opening an account takes days, our youth pricing is a maze, we have no in-app investing worth the name for this generation, and our tone of voice is a savings contract from 1987. Also, be honest about FI: anything deep in the app takes twelve to eighteen months. Anything we bolt on around it, we control ourselves."

**Heinrich Stüve, branch manager, Gütersloh (58):** "Every autumn I stand in school gyms at career fairs, next to a table of free pens. The apprentices like us — until they get their first wage and their foreman tells them he banks with ING. We are invisible at exactly the moments that decide the next forty years."

**Aylin Demir, 23, dental assistant, focus-group participant:** "I have a Sparkasse account from when I was twelve. I use it to deposit tips, that's it. My salary goes to Trade Republic — two per cent interest and my ETF plan costs nothing. Honestly? If the Sparkasse gave me a fair account and decent investing in one app, I might come back. But I'm not paying five ninety a month for a bank that sends me letters."

**Petra Vollmering, Mayor and Chair of the Verwaltungsrat (61):** "Whatever the board proposes, it must be explainable at a town-hall meeting: no branch closures smuggled in under a youth strategy, and visible engagement for schools and clubs. The Sparkasse's dividend funds our swimming pool. Its presence funds our trust."

**Dieter Hanke, Works Council Chair (55):** "Innovation, fine — but with our people, not around them. If student ambassadors and campus hubs mean new roles, we negotiate training and staffing early. Surprise us, and everything gets slower."

## 7. Seven Options on the Whiteboard

At a management offsite in August, seven candidate initiatives were sketched and roughly costed (details in Exhibit 7). They are neither mutually exclusive nor collectively exhaustive, and none has been validated:

- **A. "S-Move" youth experience layer** inside the existing Sparkasse app: a configurable young-customer mode (goals, spending insights, sub-accounts, youth content), built as an FI module plus local development.
- **B. Onboarding and switching overhaul:** account opening in under ten minutes with instant virtual card, a redesigned identification flow, and a "salary-switch concierge" that moves direct debits and informs employers.
- **C. Youth pricing move:** free current account until the 30th birthday plus 1.5% interest on balances up to €10,000 for under-30s.
- **D. "S-Invest Young":** zero-commission ETF savings plans from €10 per month and fractional investing, delivered with Deka/S-Broker, with young-customer onboarding into securities in minutes.
- **E. Regional roots programme:** three campus hubs (Bielefeld, Lemgo, Gütersloh), 25 paid student ambassadors, a structured apprentice programme with 40 *Mittelstand* employers, and financial-literacy modules in schools.
- **F. Gamified saving and budgeting:** round-ups, savings goals and challenges, and cashback with roughly 150 regional merchants.
- **G. Greenfield digital youth bank:** an own-branded neobank on a modern stack, outside FI — the "if you are serious, be serious" option championed by one board member and dreaded by the CFO.

## 8. Constraints and Guardrails

The board has set explicit guardrails for the October decision:

1. **Investment budget:** at most **€15 million cumulative one-off investment 2027-2029**.
2. **Recurring cost:** at most **€4.5 million additional recurring cost per year** in steady state (2029), net of directly attributable savings.
3. **Payback:** positive NPV at the bank's 8% planning rate with **payback within six years**.
4. **Regional principle:** growth must come from the bank's own territory — win-back, activation, and share of wallet, not national expansion.
5. **Public mandate and politics:** no reduction of the branch network as part of this programme; works council involved early; the programme must be presentable to the *Verwaltungsrat*.
6. **Platform reality:** deep changes to the core app follow FI release cycles (12-18 months); satellite builds, pricing, service, and partnerships are in the bank's own hands.

## 3. Core insight and solution sketch

**The core insight the case is engineered to reward:** this is not an "app problem" (the app is rated 4.4; Exhibit 4) and not primarily a brand problem (96% awareness, 62% crisis trust). It is a **lifecycle-pipeline problem with three mechanical leak points** — a leaky front door (funnel, Exhibit 8a), a cliff at 18 caused by parent-owned relationships (Exhibit 8b), and a value proposition that is objectively inferior on the three things young customers say drive choice: price, interest/investing, and opening speed (Exhibits 4, 5b, 5c). The strategic frame is **CLV defence**: under-30s look worthless on a one-year view (Exhibit 6) but are the sole feeder of the 30-44 mortgage cohort (71% memo item), and the *Regionalprinzip* means lost locals cannot be replaced elsewhere.

**A strong benchmark recommendation** (not the only defensible one): a phased portfolio of **B + modified C + D + E**, explicitly rejecting G, deferring A to a phase-2 decision gated on FI's release cycle, and treating F as an optional feature inside A rather than a programme.

- Phase 1 (2027): B (fix the funnel and switching) + modified C + E (be present at the moments of choice: age 18, first wage, first semester).
- Phase 2 (2027-28): D (investing is the #1 functional magnet neobanks own; delivering it via Deka/S-Broker converts the bank's securities momentum, Exhibit 3, into a youth product).
- Phase 3 (2028-29): A, scoped after FI roadmap clarity.

**The guardrail trap is intentional:** naively stacking B+C+D+E busts the recurring guardrail (0.7 + 3.4 + 0.9 + 1.3 = €6.3m > €4.5m). Strong teams *redesign* rather than drop — e.g., cap the interest offer at €5,000 (halves the €2.2m interest cost), make the fee waiver end at 27 or condition it on salary inflow (trimming forgone fees and rewarding main-bank behaviour), and phase E's hubs. A redesigned B+C+D+E package lands around €7.5m one-off (3.1 + 0.2 + 2.4 + 1.8 per Exhibit 7) and €4.0-4.5m recurring — inside all guardrails; a full app rebuild (A) stays a separately gated Phase-3 decision outside these figures, since its €6.5m one-off and €1.6m steady-state recurring would break the guardrails if bundled. Teams should also note that C's "cost" partly buys measurable main-bank inflows (salary condition), converting a giveaway into an activation lever. Rejecting G needs one slide: it violates the budget by 2.5x, repeats the YoungS lesson at scale, and its sole advantage (speed outside FI) is achievable more cheaply via satellite builds (B, D).

**Targets a good team commits to (from the data):** raise 18th-birthday retention from 59% toward 75-80% (≈ +550-700 retained per year, Exhibit 8b); raise funnel completion from 34% to ~55-60% funded (≈ +2,000-2,500 activated accounts per year on 9,400 applications, more with growth); win back dormant holders (the 71% "just in case" segment, Exhibit 5d) via salary-switch and investing — a plausible consolidated target of **+9,000 to +10,000 under-30 main-bank relationships by 2029** and main-bank share among 20-24-year-olds from 29% toward 40% (Exhibit 5a).

## 4. The quick financial model (finance lead)

The case is calibrated so a defensible model fits in 30-45 minutes of Excel:

1. **CLV of a retained/won 22-year-old.** Take the contribution path from Exhibit 6 (ages 22-24: €19; 25-29: €87; 30-44: €261; 45-59: €378), apply annual retention 96-98% and the 8% planning rate. This yields roughly **€1,100-1,500 NPV**. Adding mortgage cross-sell optionality (e.g., 30% probability × the €640 vs €95 memo differential over ten years, discounted) adds **€400-600**, for a headline of **≈ €1,500-2,100 per main-bank relationship**. Any figure in €1,200-2,500 with transparent assumptions deserves full marks; the marks are for building it, stating retention/discount assumptions, and using it consistently.
2. **Value at stake of inaction.** Net attrition ≈ 3,700 under-30 customers per year (Exhibit 1) × ≈ €1,500-2,000 ≈ **€6-7m of NPV destroyed per vintage year**, compounding. Sophisticated teams add the **funding angle**: €427m under-30 deposits at 60-90bp wholesale replacement spread = **€2.6-3.8m p.a.** of additional funding cost if the deposit base migrates — a defensive value most teams miss.
3. **Programme economics.** Redesigned B+C+D+E package ≈ €7.5m one-off + ≈ €4m net recurring (option A separately gated, not in these figures). Breakeven: roughly **6,000-8,000 incremental main-bank relationships over the plan horizon** at €1,500-2,000 CLV — then cross-check feasibility bottom-up against the three levers in Section 3 (funnel + cliff + win-back), which plausibly deliver 3,000-4,000 per year by 2029. The bottom-up/top-down triangulation is the hallmark of a top-quartile team.
4. **Sensitivities to show:** deposit margin normalising 1.7% → 1.4% (Exhibit 3 memo) cuts young-cohort NII ≈ 18%; stated-preference discount on survey conversion (see below); Trade Republic raising rates (price-war scenario — supports capping the interest offer); CIR bridge showing the programme adds ≈ 1.5-1.9 points of cost against ≈ €241m income, hence the need to state how the 68% CIR guardrail is met (income growth + existing efficiency programme, not branch cuts).

## 5. Data analysis opportunities (CS lead)

With offline tools and 8 exhibits, the CS lead can visibly differentiate the team:

- **Funnel waterfall (Exhibit 8a):** quantify each leak; show that lifting ident completion 58% → 78% and post-open funding 34% → 55-60% yields +2,000-2,500 funded accounts p.a.; design a simple A/B test plan and a KPI tree (applications → funded → salary inflow → products ≥ 2) for the roadmap slide.
- **Survey cross-analysis (Exhibit 5):** map stated switching conditions (5c) to options (C, D, B, E); apply an explicit **stated-intent discount** (e.g., only 30-50% of stated intent converts, bounded by the 4-6% market switching rate) — this single caveat, stated aloud, reliably impresses judges.
- **Segment prioritisation:** the 71% "dormant but still holding an account" group is the cheapest win-back pool (no acquisition needed, only activation); contrast CAC of activating a dormant holder vs. acquiring a neobank-native.
- **Impact-vs-cost matrix** of options A-G using Exhibits 5 and 7, making the rejection of G and deferral of A visual in one chart.

## 6. Common traps

1. **Choosing G** (greenfield neobank): budget breach, YoungS lesson, no distinctive advantage — instant credibility loss with the CFO.
2. **Stacking all options** without noticing the €4.5m recurring guardrail — the case's built-in discipline test.
3. **Price-war framing:** promising to out-yield Trade Republic; strong answers cap and condition the pricing move.
4. **Ignoring the Regionalprinzip:** any slide implying national growth or "scaling beyond the region" signals the case was not read.
5. **Branch-slashing:** violates an explicit guardrail and the *Verwaltungsrat* politics; the survey (44% cash access; 37% human advisor) shows the network is an asset to *repurpose*, not close.
6. **Blaming the app:** Exhibit 4's 4.4 rating and FI dependency are there to punish "rebuild the app" answers and reward satellite/onboarding answers.
7. **No numbers, or numbers without assumptions:** averages recited from exhibits instead of the cliff/funnel mechanics; or a CLV pulled from thin air.
8. **Implementation amnesia:** no FI timeline realism, no works-council step, no measurable 2029 targets.
