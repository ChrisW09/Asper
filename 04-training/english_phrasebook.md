# English Phrasebook for German-Speaking Presenters — ABCC 2027

**For the HSBI team and coach.** The Asper Business Case Competition (February 18–22, 2027, Winnipeg) is conducted **entirely in English**: a 25-minute prepared-case presentation with 15 minutes of Q&A, a boardroom round with a protected 3-minute recommendation followed by a 22-minute discussion with judges, a traditional case with 20 minutes plus 10 minutes of Q&A, and a 20+15 final. **Every team member must speak in every round.** Two ground rules shape the language below: never identify your school of origin to a judge (instant disqualification — you present as your drawn team letter, e.g. "Team C"), and Implementation & Resources is the heaviest rubric category (2.5x), so your English must be sharpest where you talk money, timelines, and resources. Everything here is deliberately short: under pressure you will only retrieve phrases of five to ten words.

### False friends that will hurt you

| German word | It means | The trap |
|---|---|---|
| die Milliarde | **billion** (10^9) | German "Billion" = English **trillion** (10^12). Get this wrong and your market size is off by a factor of 1,000 |
| eventuell | possibly, perhaps | "Eventually" means it *will* happen in the end ("schließlich"). "Eventually we partner" is a promise, not an option |
| der Konzern | group, corporation | English "concern" = Sorge, Anliegen ("a valid concern") |
| die Provision | commission | English "provision" = Rückstellung — an accounting term. Sales reps earn *commission* |
| sensibel | sensitive | "Sensible" = vernünftig. Customers are *price-sensitive*; a plan is *sensible* |
| aktuell | current | "Actually" = tatsächlich. Say "currently", never "actually" for "zurzeit" |
| konsequent | consistent, rigorous | "Consequently" = folglich |
| bekommen | to get, to receive | "To become" = werden. You *get* a discount; you do not become one |
| der Chef | boss, CEO | A "chef" cooks |

## 2. Presentation phrase bank

**Openings.** State the recommendation within the first minute — judges score "Framing the problem". Never name your school; you are your team letter.

- "Good morning, and thank you for the opportunity to present."
- "We are Team C, and over the next twenty minutes we will show you how [Client] can [headline result]."
- "Our recommendation in one sentence: [X]. Here is why — and how."
- "[Client] faces one core question: [question]. Our answer is yes, and it pays for itself in [n] months."
- "Three things today: the problem, our answer, and exactly how to make it happen."

**Signposting.** One signpost per section; keep it to a single breath.

- "Let me start with the problem."
- "That brings me to the financials."
- "So much for the market — now the money."
- "The key takeaway from this slide is simple: [X]."
- "Here is where it gets interesting."
- "We looked at three options and kept one."
- "And now the part that matters most: implementation."

**Handovers between speakers.** All four members must speak, so you will hand over at least three times. One sentence out, half a sentence in — never re-introduce the deck.

- "With that, I will hand over to Anna, who will walk you through the financials."
- "For the numbers behind this, over to Jonas."
- "Lena will now show you how we de-risk the rollout."
- Receiving: "Thank you, Maria. As she said, the margin is the lever — here is how we pull it."
- Receiving: "Thanks, Jonas. Building on that market picture, ..."

**Closing the ask.** End on implementation, cost, and payback — the heaviest rubric line. The yellow card means five minutes left; the red card means one.

- "To sum up: we recommend [X]. It costs [Y], breaks even in [Z], and the first milestone is in April."
- "This is feasible with the team and budget [Client] already has."
- "The ask today: approve [X] and a budget of [Y], and we start in Q2."
- "If you remember one number, make it this one: [n]."
- Under the red card: "I see the red card — let me close with the essentials: [three short points]. Thank you. We are happy to take your questions."

## 3. Q&A under fire

Q&A runs 10–15 minutes depending on the round, in front of industry judges. Rule one: **answer first, explain second.** Rule two: short beats complete.

**Answer-first stems**

- "Yes — and here is why."
- "No, and deliberately so: ..."
- "The short answer is [X]. The reasoning: ..."
- "Two things. First ... second ..."
- "It depends on one variable: [X]. If it rises, we ..."
- "We tested exactly that. The result was ..."

**Bridging** (when the question is hostile, narrow, or off-track)

- "That touches on our second risk. Our mitigation is ..."
- "The underlying question is whether [X] — and our answer is yes, because ..."
- "Fair point on the detail; the bigger picture is ..."
- "Let me answer that and then connect it back to the recommendation."

**Polite disagreement** (disagree with the premise, never with the person)

- "That is a fair challenge. We saw it differently, and here is why."
- "I would push back gently on one assumption there."
- "We considered that option and ruled it out, for two reasons."
- "You are right about the risk; we differ on its size."
- Conceding well: "That is a good catch — our estimate is conservative there. The recommendation still holds because ..."

**Buying two seconds without "ähm"**

- "That is a fair question."
- "Good question — let me take the financing part first."
- Repeat the key noun back: "The rollout timeline. Two points."
- Or simply pause, breathe, then answer. Two seconds of silence read as composure; two seconds of "ähm" read as panic.
- Banned fillers (they slip out in English too): "ähm", "genau", "also", "sozusagen", and the tag "…or?" (a direct translation of "oder?" — it does not exist in English).

**When you missed the question acoustically**

- "I'm sorry, I didn't catch the last part — could you repeat it?"
- "Could you say that again? I want to make sure I answer the right question."
- Paraphrase check: "If I understood correctly, you are asking about [X] — is that right?"
- Teammate rescue (agree on this beforehand): "I believe the question was about [X] — I can take that."
- Never bluff an answer you did not hear. Asking once is professional; guessing wrong is not.

## 4. Boardroom register

The boardroom round is different: after your protected three minutes (one summary slide, uninterrupted), you sit through a **22-minute discussion** with the judges — in past editions including a company representative. This is a conversation, not a lecture, and every member must speak during it. The register is collegial and interactive.

**Inviting**

- "Happy to go deeper on the numbers if that is useful."
- "May I ask how you see the trade-off between [X] and [Y]?"
- "What would need to be true for this to be a yes for you?"
- "Is there a part of the plan you would like us to stress-test?"

**Building**

- "To build on that point ..."
- "Exactly — and that is why we phased the rollout."
- "That connects to what Jonas said earlier about capacity."
- "Yes — and one number supports that: ..."

**Redirecting**

- "That is an important point — may I link it back to the margin question?"
- "Before we leave the financials, one number I would like on the table: ..."
- "We are conscious of time, so let me summarize where we have landed."
- "Can I park that for a moment and come back to it? I want to answer the funding question first."

**Bringing in a teammate** (use this deliberately — it is how everyone gets speaking time)

- "Lena owns this part of the model — Lena?"
- "I will let our finance lead take that."
- "Maria ran that analysis; she can give you the exact figure."

## 5. Numbers out loud

Numbers are where German habits are most audible. Six rules:

1. **Decimal point, not comma.** German 3,5 is English 3.5 — "three point five." Thousands separators flip too: 1.500.000 becomes 1,500,000.
2. **Percent never takes a plural.** "Seven percent," not "seven percents." A *change* between two percentages is measured in **percentage points**: "up three points, from 9 to 12 percent."
3. **Currency after the number when speaking.** Written "$1.5m," spoken "one point five million dollars." First mention in Winnipeg: "Canadian dollars"; after that, "dollars."
4. **Magnitudes:** Million = million; **Milliarde = billion; Billion = trillion.** Drill this until it is reflex.
5. **Years and dates:** 2027 is "twenty twenty-seven." February 18 is "February eighteenth." Fiscal year 2026 is "FY twenty-six."
6. **Negatives and ranges:** "minus" or "down": "NPV falls to minus 340,000 dollars"; "between five and seven percent" or "five to seven percent."

**Twelve practice sentences.** Read aloud daily; record yourself weekly. Spoken forms of the traps are in brackets.

1. Revenue grew by 4.7% ["four point seven percent"] to $23.5 million ["twenty-three point five million dollars"].
2. Gross margin improved from 38.2% to 41.5% — an increase of 3.3 percentage points.
3. The project requires an upfront investment of $1.2 million and breaks even after 14 months.
4. We assume a discount rate of 8% and a terminal growth rate of 1.5%.
5. The Canadian market is worth 2.3 billion Canadian dollars [Milliarden!], growing at a CAGR ["C-A-G-R"] of 6.1%.
6. Unit costs fall from $4.60 ["four dollars sixty" or "four sixty"] to $3.95, a saving of roughly 14%.
7. In the worst case, NPV drops to minus $340,000 ["three hundred and forty thousand dollars"].
8. Phase one runs from March to June 2027 ["twenty twenty-seven"]; phase two starts on July 1st ["July first"].
9. Two-thirds of customers are under 35, and one in four buys online.
10. Headcount rises from 48 to 63 FTEs — that is, by about 31%.
11. The debt-to-equity ratio is 1.8 to 1 ["one point eight to one"].
12. Sales tripled between 2024 and 2026 — from $0.9 million ["zero point nine" or "nine hundred thousand dollars"] to $2.7 million.

## 6. Pronunciation traps for German speakers

**th.** Two sounds — never s, z, t, or d. Tongue touches the teeth. Voiceless /θ/: *think, three, third, thousand, threshold, growth, monthly, strengths, worth.* Voiced /ð/: *this, that, therefore, rather, either, whether.* Drill line: "We think this third growth path is worth three thousand."

**v vs. w.** Germans swap in both directions. **v** = upper teeth on lower lip: *value, invest, revenue, viable, leverage, review, innovation.* **w** = rounded lips, no teeth: *we, would, win, workforce, worldwide, quarter* (kw-). Minimal pairs: *vest/west, vine/wine, verse/worse.* Drill line: "We will invest in value this winter."

**-ed endings.** Three variants — and beware German final devoicing (Auslautverhärtung), which turns "planned" into "plant":

- /t/ after voiceless sounds: *launched, increased, priced, reduced, fixed*
- /d/ after voiced sounds — keep the d voiced: *planned, positioned, delivered, phased, designed*
- /ɪd/ as an extra syllable only after t or d: *implemented, expected, invested, decided, targeted*

Never add /ɪd/ elsewhere: "launched" has one syllable, not two.

**Word stress.** English moves stress within word families; German speakers tend to keep it fixed.

- **STRAT**-egy, but stra-**TE**-gic
- **AN**-a-lyze, a-**NAL**-y-sis, plural a-**NAL**-y-ses (ends in "-seez" /siːz/ — different from the singular)
- e-**CON**-o-my, ec-o-**NOM**-ics, ec-o-**NOM**-i-cal
- **COM**-pe-tent, com-**PET**-i-tor, com-pe-**TI**-tion
- de-**VEL**-op-ment (never "develop-MENT"), per-**CENT**, ho-**TEL**, e-**VENT**
- Noun vs. verb: an **IN**-crease / to in-**CREASE**; a **RE**-cord / to re-**CORD**; a **PRO**-ject (noun — the verb is rare)

**Silent letters and finance-specific words.** *debt* = "det" (silent b), *receipt* = "re-SEET" (silent p), *doubt, subtle* (silent b), *margin* with a soft g ("MAR-jin"), *niche* ("neesh" or "nitch" — pick one and stay consistent), *suite* = "sweet" (a hotel suite; a *suit* is what you wear), *hierarchy* = "HIGH-er-ar-kee", *says* = "sez".

**Daily two-minute drill.** Run this word ladder before every mock presentation: *three — this — growth — strengths — value — we — viable — workforce — launched — planned — implemented — strategy — strategic — analysis — analyses — competitor — development — percent — debt — margin.* Then one full sentence: "Our strategic analysis shows a viable path to three percent growth, with the debt repaid by twenty twenty-nine."
