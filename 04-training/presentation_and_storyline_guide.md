# Presentation and Storyline Guide

**Core skills trio, part 1 of 3 · Delivery: how the argument reaches the judges.** The other two: [`qa_drills.md`](qa_drills.md) · [`business_math_drills.md`](business_math_drills.md). Folder overview: [`README.md`](README.md).

This guide is for our four-member Masters team (finance, innovation, computer science, general business administration) and our coach. It covers how we build and deliver decks across all three Asper rounds: the **Prepared case** (received ~2 weeks ahead; 25-minute presentation + 15-minute Q&A; 25% of total score), the **Boardroom case** (4-hour prep; 3-minute pitch + 22-minute seated discussion; 30%), and the **Traditional case** (20-minute presentation + 10-minute Q&A; 45%) — 3-hour prep through 2026, and in **2027 a 10-hour case** (prep Fri Feb 19, presentations and finals Sat Feb 20; working assumption until the organizers confirm — see `01-competition/facts_and_rules.md`, "What 2027 changed"). Asper's judges are industry practitioners, not academics — and the official ABCC rubric (2025 Event Guide) makes **Implementation & Resources the single heaviest criterion at 2.5×**, ahead of Analysis (2.0×) and Recommendation (2.0×), with communication, framing, impact, and risk categories at 1.0× each. Everything below is tuned to that reality: one decisive, quantified, executable recommendation with a resourced roadmap.

## 1. Action titles and horizontal logic

**The rule.** Every slide title is a full-sentence claim — with a number wherever possible — never a label. The slide body exists only to prove its title (vertical logic). Read top to bottom, the titles alone must tell the complete argument (horizontal logic). Judges skim; many will form their verdict from titles and charts alone.

| Weak (label) | Strong (action title) |
| --- | --- |
| Market Overview | The Canadian premium candle market grows 6% p.a. — but 70% of value sits in gifting occasions |
| Financial Projections | A CAD 1.2M investment returns CAD 3.4M NPV and pays back in 2.1 years |
| Risks | The two risks that matter — channel conflict and FX — are capped at CAD 0.4M by our phasing |

**The 60-second test.** Before any rehearsal, one member reads only the titles aloud. If a listener cannot reproduce the recommendation, its three reasons, the cost, and the timeline from titles alone, we rewrite titles before touching anything else.

**Slide discipline.** One idea per slide; chart-led, not text-led; a judge should grasp each visual in under five seconds; two to three colors, consistent throughout. Quantify every claim: "raises EBITDA margin 3.2 pp, adding CAD 4.7M by 2029" beats "improves profitability" every time. Rough estimates with disclosed assumptions beat vague adjectives.

## 2. The standard storyline skeleton

We use one skeleton for every deck, pre-built in our branded template (logos, colors, layouts — no text, as competition rules require). Pyramid Principle throughout: answer first, proof after. One recommendation, never a menu — "We recommend B, and here is why A and C don't hold up."

| # | Slide | Job of the slide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Title | Team identity, client, date; recommendation teased in the subtitle |
| 2 | Executive summary | The recommendation, three supporting reasons, quantified impact, the ask |
| 3 | Situation and problem | The one-sentence exam question and why it is urgent now |
| 4–6 | Analysis | One insight per slide proving reasons 1–3 (market, customer, financial logic) |
| 7 | Recommendation detail | What exactly we will do, for whom, at what price and scope |
| 8 | Financials | Investment, returns, NPV/payback, sensitivity band |
| 9–10 | Implementation | 30/60/90-day plan plus a phased multi-year roadmap with owners, resources, milestones |
| 11 | Risks and mitigations | Top three risks, each with a mitigation and a trigger point |
| 12 | Close | Recommendation and impact restated in one line |
| A1–A15+ | Appendix | Numbered Q&A ammunition: model detail, sensitivities, Gantt, sources |

Implementation gets two slides because practitioner judges score execution hardest. The appendix is a weapon, not a dump: every appendix slide is built to answer a predicted question and is numbered so we can call it up in seconds.

## 3. The 3-hour production workflow (Traditional round)

Principles proven by winning teams: hypothesis-driven from minute one; roughly 15% reading, 25% structured debate, 50% parallel build, 10–15% rehearsal; hard gates that are never reopened; slides built while analysis happens, not afterwards.

**Roles.**

| Role | Team member profile | Owns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quarterback (PM) | General business administration | Time, storyline, executive summary, implementation slides, final decisions |
| Chief Analyst | Finance | The model, every case number, financial and sensitivity slides |
| Strategist | Innovation | Alternatives, recommendation design, risks, the creative edge |
| Design & Data Lead | Computer science | Template, charts, deck assembly, appendix index, "slide DJ" in Q&A |

**Minute by minute.**

| Time | Quarterback | Chief Analyst | Strategist | Design & Data Lead |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–0:05 | Reads the prompt aloud twice; writes the exam question on the whiteboard | Opens model shell | Recalls industry priors from our prepared country/industry files | Opens template and shared deck; sets skeleton |
| 0:05–0:30 | Silent read: prompt, stakeholders, constraints, decision-maker | Silent read: exhibits and financials; tags the page of every figure | Silent read: market, customers, competition | Silent read: chartable data, operations facts |
| 0:30–0:35 | Runs readouts: each member states the problem in one sentence plus top three facts | — | — | — |
| 0:35–0:55 | Facilitates whiteboard debate in fixed order: problem → key issues → alternatives → pros/cons → pick ONE | Sizes each alternative roughly | Argues the alternatives; proposes the winner | Scribes the whiteboard |
| **0:55** | **GATE 1: recommendation locked. Never reopened.** | | | |
| 0:55–1:05 | Writes the 12 action titles on the whiteboard; team approves; sections assigned | Confirms which numbers each title needs | Sharpens title wording | Types titles into the deck |
| 1:05–2:20 | Drafts executive summary shell and implementation slides; calls time every 15 minutes; kills drift aloud | Builds model → financial slides → sensitivity appendix (numbers locked by 1:30) | Drafts recommendation and risk slides; writes the one-liners | Builds charts on request; polishes each section as it lands; builds the appendix index |
| **2:00** | **GATE 2: every draft slide is in the deck.** | | | |
| 2:20–2:35 | Title-only read-through; cuts orphan slides | Verifies every number against the model | Checks story flow | Consistency scrub: fonts, colors, footers, numbering |
| 2:35–2:55 | Rehearsal 1 (rough, with handovers), then Rehearsal 2 (timed, polished); after each, every member fires three hardballs | — | — | — |
| 2:55–3:00 | Freeze; export PDF + PPTX + USB; restroom; pre-room ritual | — | — | — |

Rules that make it work: the Quarterback vocalizes time and drift ("Twenty minutes gone — we are still on issues"); consensus always, but the Quarterback breaks ties instantly; anyone who finishes early builds appendix ammunition; rehearsal time is sacred — an average deck delivered brilliantly beats a brilliant deck delivered raw. For the 4-hour Boardroom round, stretch the build block and add a Q&A-only rehearsal. For the Prepared round, run this same skeleton, then iterate it with the coach across the two weeks before travel — remember there is no coach contact once cases are released on site, and no phones or smartwatches in the prep room.

### Stretching it to the 10-hour case (2027 third round)

The 2027 schedule replaces the 3-hour traditional prep with a **10-hour case** — same 20 + 10 delivery, same 45 % weight (assumed until confirmed). The 3-hour workflow above stays the **core**: the same roles, the same order (read → debate → lock → titles → build → rehearse), the same two gates. What changes is what fills the extra seven hours, and what judges will expect from a team that had them.

**What judges will expect that they forgive at 3 hours.**

- **Primary-style research.** Real market data, competitor pricing, comparable cases, regulatory facts — sourced on the slide, not assumed. "We estimate" becomes "we checked".
- **Fuller financials.** A three-scenario model with explicit drivers, a sensitivity on the two assumptions that matter, payback/NPV *and* the funding source. Every number traceable in the appendix.
- **A sharper implementation plan** (the 2.5× criterion). Named owners, month-by-month phasing, budget by phase, KPIs with targets and dates, a stop/go gate — no generic "Phase 1 / 2 / 3".
- **An appendix bank.** 15–25 numbered appendix slides, each answering a predicted question, indexed for the slide DJ.
- **More rehearsal, not more slides.** The main deck still fits 20 minutes; the extra time goes into proof and polish.

**The 10-hour clock** — the team-level view of the authoritative clock in `03-team/role_playbooks.md` ("The 10-hour day"; per-role detail in its 10-hour columns). Times assume an 08:30 release.

| Time | Block |
| --- | --- |
| 0:00–0:45 | Read and frame — as in the 3-hour workflow; key facts, exhibits and the decision-maker's question logged |
| 0:45–1:30 | Align and decide — a *provisional* recommendation plus a written research plan (the 5–8 questions that could change the decision, each with an owner) |
| 1:30–3:30 | Research and first analysis — desk research timeboxed at 30 min per thread, benchmarks into the source log, first model pass |
| **3:30** | **GATE A: direction confirmed** — the last free chance to change the recommendation; paper storyboard agreed |
| 3:50–4:20 | **Lunch, all four, away from screens** — scheduled, not optional |
| 4:20–6:00 | Deep analysis — full model with sensitivities, implementation plan (owners, budget, timeline, first 90 days), KPIs and risks, alternatives considered |
| **6:00** | **GATE B: storyline frozen** — every slide has its action title; ghost deck in the template |
| 6:00–8:00 | Build — rolling merge; appendix bank built in parallel; a 15-minute food break around 7:00 |
| 8:00–8:30 | Number audit — every displayed figure recomputed |
| **8:30** | **Content and number freeze** |
| 8:30–9:30 | Full timed rehearsal (20 + 10 with cards), then the AI Q&A-anticipation routine |
| 9:30–10:00 | Lockdown and USB, then buffer — never spent on new content |

Rules that change at 10 hours: energy is a resource, so food, water and the breaks are on the clock before the day starts; the gates come **later but are still never reopened** after Gate A; and because presentations fall on the following day (Sat Feb 20), the deck is frozen at the end of prep — the evening is for dinner, one read of the Q&A grid and sleep, not edits. We first run this at W17 (Sparkasse case) and again in the W25 competition-week simulation.

## 4. The 3-minute boardroom pitch formula

Three minutes is roughly 400 words at our deliberate non-native pace (about 135 words per minute). One summary slide only — appendices are blocked during the protected 3 minutes and accessible only in the 22-minute discussion; everything else lives in the appendix. This is the one script we memorize verbatim and never improvise.

| Clock | Beat | Content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–0:20 | Governing thesis | "We recommend [action]. It delivers [quantified impact] by [date] for an investment of [X]." |
| 0:20–0:50 | The problem, sharpened | The single insight that reframes the case — why now, in one number |
| 0:50–2:10 | Three pillars | Reasons 1–3, ~25 seconds each, one number each, one speaker each |
| 2:10–2:40 | Money and motion | Investment, return, payback, and the first 90 days |
| 2:40–3:00 | Risk plus invitation | "The main risk is [X]; we cap it by [Y]. We would like to use this discussion to pressure-test three areas with you: [A], [B], [C]." |

The closing invitation is not decoration. Board-presentation research is unambiguous: directors engage when you ask for their input on named decision points, and disengage when you fish for applause.

## 5. Seated boardroom discussion technique (22 minutes)

This is a conversation among professionals, not a defended thesis. The team that behaves like colleagues of the judges wins the room.

- **Everyone speaks, by design.** Competitions in this format rank teams last if a member stays silent. Pre-assign domains: Quarterback frames and closes; Chief Analyst owns every number; Strategist owns market and competitive logic; Design & Data Lead owns customer, data, and technology questions.
- **Plant the agenda.** The three pressure-test areas named in the pitch give judges a menu; the discussion usually starts there — on ground we chose.
- **Appendix as ammunition.** The Design & Data Lead acts as slide DJ: appendix slides are numbered and indexed on a single sheet, and the right exhibit is on screen within five seconds of a question ("Appendix 7 shows exactly that sensitivity").
- **Build, don't defend.** Open answers with "Yes, and —" or "Fair — and here is how we handled it," never with "No." Use judges' names if they were introduced. Note their points visibly on paper and reference them later ("Coming back to your margin concern…"). When a judge starts nodding, finish the sentence and stop.
- **Re-anchor.** Every three or four exchanges, someone ties the thread back to the governing thesis in one line. If offered a final word, the Quarterback closes with a 20-second restatement: recommendation, impact, first step.
- **Posture.** Forearms on the table, lean slightly in, notepad out, no laptops open except the DJ's.

## 6. Handovers

Sloppy transitions are a documented failure mode at every major competition — and the cheapest one to fix.

**The baton formula.** The outgoing speaker closes their point and opens the next section with the teammate's name; the incoming speaker re-anchors in one line:

> "…so the market is ready. How much value that creates — Lena has the numbers."
> "Thanks, Jonas. Three numbers matter: 1.2 million invested, 3.4 million returned, 2.1 years to payback."

Rules: never end a section on a filler sentence; the clicker travels during the name, not after it; the incoming speaker steps forward as the name is spoken — motion signals structure; equal airtime for all four members in every round; rehearse handovers as their own drill — five consecutive clean passes before any full run-through counts.

## 7. Delivery for non-native speakers

Our English is an asset if we manage it deliberately.

- **Slow is credible.** Target 130–140 words per minute. Pause instead of filling; silence reads as confidence, "ähm" does not.
- **Short sentences.** German builds long nested clauses; spoken English rewards subject–verb–object. Cap spoken sentences at roughly 15 words.
- **Kill the Germanisms.** "We would like to present" → "We will show." "Until Friday" → "by Friday." "Actual" is not "current." "Chance" → "opportunity." And the dangerous false friend: German *Billion* = English *trillion*; *Milliarde* = billion. Practice numbers aloud in English daily — decimal point, not comma: "two point one years."
- **Memorize the load-bearing lines verbatim:** the first 30 seconds, every handover, the closing line, and the one-sentence recommendation. Improvise the middle, never the frame.
- **Drill problem sounds** (th, v/w, -ed endings) in warm-ups. Record every rehearsal and review for pace and fillers — "basically," "actually," and "you know" are banned words.
- **Never apologize for your English.** No "sorry for my accent" — ever. Structure and precision outrank pronunciation.

## 8. Dress and stage presence

- **Attire.** Business formal, coordinated as a team, harmonized with the deck's color scheme. Pack two complete outfits plus a stain-and-repair kit; competition weeks are long, and a crisp look on stage is part of the score.
- **Stance.** Standing: weight balanced, no pacing, no lectern-gripping; gestures at chest height; eye contact swept across all judges, not only the friendly one. Seated (boardroom): upright, hands visible, forearms on the table.
- **Energy.** Practitioner judges read presence in the first 30 seconds. Smile at the open and the close. Manage fuel deliberately — water and snacks during prep, nothing experimental on competition days — and protect sleep: competitors consistently report visible delivery collapse after short-sleep nights.
- **Logistics as presence.** Visit the presentation room before round one; know where the restrooms are so no prep minute is lost; arrive early enough to breathe. Run the fixed pre-room ritual: 60 seconds, top three keys spoken aloud, one breath together, walk in.
- **After the buzzer.** Leave the outcome in the room. No celebration before the final, no post-mortems before the next case. Reset, refuel, go again.
